## How to read this

**Importance** uses four levels:

- **Essential** — I'd consider this a necessity. I wouldn't ship without it.
- **Recommended** — clear, real payoff. Most firms should do it.
- **Optional** — helps, but situational.
- **Low priority** — common to offer, but honestly weak fit for an engineering firm.

## Honest descriptions (the pitch voice)

**1. Visual redesign.** Essential. The site today is competent but reads like a polished template. The redesign moves it to sharp, drafted geometry — square corners, thin gold rules, a blueprint grid, consistent duotone photos — so it looks like a firm that measures things *exactly*. This is the foundation everything else sits on, and it's the single biggest jump in perceived professionalism. You already have the brand colors and fonts; this is execution, not invention.

**2 & 3. (Removed — my own cleanup, no charge.)** The homepage-only footer, leftover template code, the wrong-domain robots file, and missing page metadata were all my earlier mistakes. I fix them for free as part of any redesign — they don't go on Mitch's bill, and they don't go in his guide.

**4. Local SEO + structured data.** Highly beneficial — with an honest caveat. Local search rewards a consistent Name/Address/Phone and a Google Business Profile, and **right now there's no public business address.** We can run Cornerstone as a "service-area business" (no street address shown) and still add `LocalBusiness` structured data, which helps Google understand who you are and what you do. But be straight with him: without an address or a claimed Business Profile, the ceiling on local ranking is lower. If he *can* share an office address and claim a Business Profile, this jumps from "Recommended" toward "Essential."

**5. Contact form.** Recommended. Today a visitor has to copy an email address and switch to their mail app — friction that loses leads. A simple form with spam protection captures the people who wouldn't have bothered, and it routes straight to his inbox. Low effort, real lead capture.

**6. Projects / portfolio.** Recommended, and underrated. Engineering buyers — developers, municipalities, builders — trust *proof of built work* more than adjectives. A clean, consistent project grid (the photo strip in the mockup) does more for credibility than any amount of copy. The main input needed is photos and a short blurb per project.

**7. Service-page content expansion.** Recommended. Each service is currently one or two sentences. Giving civil engineering, land surveying, project management, and sustainable design their own real sections does two jobs at once: it gives Google enough content to rank each service for its keywords, and it answers the questions a serious client actually has. This one is writing-heavy, which is why it's a couple of days.

**8. Performance / Core Web Vitals.** Recommended. Google uses page speed as a ranking signal, and a few images aren't optimized yet. Moderate effort, steady payoff — and it makes the site feel snappier, which reinforces the "precision" impression.

**9. Analytics + Search Console.** Essential and cheap. You can't improve what you can't measure. Without this, every later decision is a guess. Search Console also tells us when Google has indexed the fixes from #3. Half a day, set-and-forget.

**10. Accessibility pass.** Optional-to-recommended. Part of it is simply the right thing to do; part of it overlaps with SEO; and part of it is the appearance of professionalism for public-facing or municipal clients who care about it. Not urgent, but a clean, cheap credibility item.

**11. Blog / news.** Low priority — and I'd say so to his face. A blog is the standard "do this for SEO" recommendation, and for many businesses it drives traffic. For an engineering/surveying firm whose clients are a small set of developers and agencies, it's a weak lead driver. Its real value is *looking active and credible* — a "Firm News" page for new hires, project wins, or licenses. Worth it only if someone will actually keep it updated; a stale blog looks worse than none.

**12. Admin dashboard.** Essential *if his goal is to stop paying per change* — which it is. This is the biggest website line because it's real software, not styling: it needs a secure login, a place to store the content, and editable screens for the things that change (team roster, services, contact info, hero text, licensing). Be honest that it's more than a quick add-on. But framed correctly, it pays for itself: every future text or team change becomes free for him instead of a billable task. Full architecture options are in the New NET document, since the same stack can power both. *(Note: the $500 placeholder in your earlier brief was a format example — a real CMS-backed admin is genuinely a 1.5–2.5 week build.)*

**13. Care plan.** Recommended. A small monthly retainer that covers hosting oversight, security/dependency updates, backups, and a bucket of minor changes. Predictable for him, recurring for you. If he buys the dashboard he'll need less of this, but he'll still want someone watching the lights.

**14. Google Business Profile setup + optimization.** Essential for local visibility, and a perfect fit for a delegate-everything client. This is the free Google listing that shows up in Maps and the local "pack" of results — it's the single biggest lever for a local firm getting found, and it's the other half of item #4 (local SEO). I set it up, verify it, fill in services and hours, and make sure the Name/Address/Phone matches the site exactly. Mitch just has to approve the verification. Low cost, high local payoff.

**15. Email migration off Bluehost.** Recommended — and it solves a real, daily staff complaint. **The reassuring part: nobody loses their email and nobody's address changes.** Mail lives in mailboxes on the server, copied to the new provider over a standard protocol (IMAP) *before* anything is cancelled; the domain stays put, we just re-point a few DNS records. The only way to lose mail is to cancel Bluehost first — so we never do that. I'd move them to **Google Workspace** or **Microsoft 365** (comparison below), configure DNS + spam-deliverability records (SPF/DKIM/DMARC), migrate calendars/contacts, and verify before switching off Bluehost. The monthly per-seat cost is Mitch's, paid to Google/Microsoft directly (pass-through, no markup).

### Email provider comparison (verified June 2026 pricing, per user/month)

| Plan | Cost (annual) | Desktop Office apps | Cloud storage / user | Best for |
|------|---------------|---------------------|----------------------|----------|
| **Google Workspace — Business Starter** | $7 | No (web only) | 30 GB | Simplest upgrade; Gmail-style |
| **Google Workspace — Business Standard** | $14 | No (web only) | 2 TB | More storage + better video meetings |
| **Microsoft 365 — Business Basic** | $6 (→$7 Jul 1) | No (web only) | 1 TB OneDrive | Cheapest; Outlook + Teams |
| **Microsoft 365 — Business Standard** | $12.50 (→$14 Jul 1) | **Yes** (Outlook, Word, Excel desktop) | 1 TB OneDrive | A firm that lives in Excel + wants desktop Office |

For ~15 staff, that's roughly **$90–210/month** depending on plan. My lean: an engineering firm that lives in Excel and may want to reduce pCloud usage is a strong fit for **Microsoft 365 Business Standard** (desktop Office + 1 TB OneDrive each + SharePoint). If they prefer simple and web-first, **Google Workspace** is the easier admin experience.

> **Timing note worth telling Mitch:** Microsoft raises 365 prices on **July 1, 2026**, but existing customers keep current rates until renewal. If M365 is the pick, signing up before then locks the lower price. (Today is June 13, 2026 — small window.)

**Billing model (so the cost is exact):** Both Google and Microsoft bill **per user/account**, not per email address. One license = one person with their own mailbox and login. Extra addresses are **free aliases** (Google allows up to 30 per user — e.g. `info@`, `sales@`, `billing@` all funneling into one paid inbox), and shared team inboxes can be free Groups. So ~15 staff ≈ **15 licenses**, no matter how many addresses exist.

**The `cesinbox.com` situation.** Mitch currently pays for a *second* domain, `cesinbox.com`, purely to host the company's email (that's the `info@cesinbox.com` on the contact page) — the website lives on `cornerstoneengineeringandsurveying.com`. When we migrate, the domain we re-point is **`cesinbox.com`**, and there's an opportunity worth raising: with Workspace/365 we can run email on the real brand domain (`you@cornerstoneengineeringandsurveying.com`) — more professional — and keep every `cesinbox.com` address alive as a **free alias** so nothing breaks. That could let him **drop the separate `cesinbox.com` domain cost** entirely.

**What Workspace adds over Bluehost** (maps to the staff's "lacks functionality" complaints): far better search and spam/deliverability, much more storage, shared calendars with scheduling, real-time Docs/Sheets/Slides + shared Drives, built-in Meet video calls, working mobile apps, a real admin console (enforce 2FA; when someone leaves, suspend them and transfer their mail + files to a colleague — not cleanly possible on Bluehost), and bundled Gemini AI.
